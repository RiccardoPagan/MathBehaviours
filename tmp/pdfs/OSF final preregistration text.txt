From Attitudes to Behaviour: How Math Attitudes Influence Students’ Behavioural Choices 
in Primary School 
 
Overview 
Research questions or hypotheses 
To date, mathematics-related behaviors such as avoidance have primarily been studied through 
self-report measures or long-term outcomes like course selection and career choices (Pizzie & 
Kraemer, 2017). However, in primary school, where opting out of mathematics is not feasible, 
alternative approaches are needed to capture students' real-time behavioural choices (Hilz et 
al., 2025). The present study introduces an innovative behavioral measure by observing choices 
during a mathematical task, making it exploratory for its ordinal structure while hypothesizing 
monotonic task engagement from lower (skipping) to higher (self-reliance) levels. For further 
details about study design and method, see the section below. The aim of this study is to 
investigate the two following closely related research questions within an ordinal modelling 
framework, for which statistical power is planned. RQ1 examines how trait- and state-level 
math attitudes predict those choices and whether state-level attitudes mediate the effects of 
their trait-level counterparts, whereas RQ2 concerns the ordinal structure of behavioural 
choices. The two questions are therefore considered together, as they address the same 
behavioural outcome from complementary perspectives. RQ1: Whether the following math 
attitudes (avoidance, buoyancy, worry and perceived competence) predict students’ 
behavioural choices during the mathematical task (i.e., skipping, help-seeking, and self-reliant 
responding), controlling for gender and math ability, and do state-level attitudes statistically 
account for part of the association between their corresponding trait-level measures and these 
choices? HP1: Given the multiple data-dependent decisions involved, the analyses addressing 
RQ1 are best characterised as inferentially exploratory rather than strictly confirmatory. 
Specifically, directional hypotheses are preregistered a priori, but their formal testing depends 
on a sequence of model-selection and predictor-screening decisions that cannot be fully fixed 
in advance. We therefore treat the final p-values as conditional on the selected model and 
acknowledge that this procedure provides only imperfect control over false positive results. 
Specifically, while the direction of expected effects is grounded in converging evidence (Choe et 
al., 2019; Putwain et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025), the final set of predictors, the mediation 
structure, and the response parameterisation cannot be fully determined in advance. Predictor 
inclusion will follow the model-building procedure described in the “Statistical models” section. 
Math anxiety and self-competence are hypothesised to influence behavioural choices primarily 
through their state-level counterparts (math anxiety via worry, and self-competence via 
perceived competence), whereas buoyancy and avoidance, if psychometrically adequate, are 
expected to contribute directly to choice. After accounting for prior math ability and gender, 
students with more adaptive motivational-affective profiles are expected to show greater task 
engagement. Specifically, higher academic buoyancy and higher perceived competence are 
expected to predict more engaged behavioural choices (i.e., self-reliant responding), whereas 
higher avoidance and higher worry are expected to predict less engaged choices (i.e., skipping). 
Prior math ability from T0 and gender will be included as covariates in all models to distinguish 
ability-driven from attitude-driven variation in choices. The two-stage model-building procedure 
described in the Statistical models section is designed to detect and accommodate such 
patterns. RQ2: Within the ordinal representation of students’ behavioural choices during the 
mathematical task (skipping < help-seeking < self-reliance), do some predictors require 

PAGE 2
category-specific effects across ordinal transitions? HP2: We expect students’ behavioural 
choices to be compatible with an ordered representation of task engagement, in which skipping 
reflects lower engagement, help-seeking an intermediate form, and self-reliant responding 
higher engagement. These effects are expected to be monotonic across the ordinal continuum 
from skipping to help-seeking to self-reliance. However, we acknowledge that the proportional-
odds assumption may not hold uniformly for all predictors: some attitude variables may 
differentiate more strongly between specific transitions than others, warranting category-
specific parameterisation for those predictors. If the proportional-odds assumption is not 
supported for one or more predictors, category-specific ordinal parameterisations will be 
estimated. Inferential tests will be conducted only after model selection has been completed 
according to the preregistered criteria. All inferential results will therefore be explicitly reported 
as conditional on the selected model and should not be interpreted as if the final model had 
been fixed a priori.

Study design 
The study employs two sessions (T0 and T1) to examine the relationships between math 
attitudes (both trait- and state-level), math behavioural choice, and math ability. At T0, 

PAGE 3
participants complete a first block of 16 arithmetic problems, followed by two state 
questionnaires assessing worry and perceived competence. The session then continues with a 
second block of 16 arithmetic problems. At the end of the session, participants complete two 
trait questionnaires assessing math anxiety and academic buoyancy. At T1, participants 
complete 32 arithmetic problems divided into two equal blocks. Unlike T0, each problem is 
presented together with three response options corresponding to different behavioural choices: 
participants may skip the item and move on to the next one, request a hint before attempting 
the problem, or solve the problem independently. After completing both blocks of the math 
task, participants complete the remaining two trait questionnaires assessing math avoidance 
and math self-competence. All tasks are administered by computer in small groups within the 
classroom. For both RQ1 and RQ2, the primary outcome variable is students’ item-level 
behavioural choice during the math task (i.e., skipping, help-seeking, or self-reliance 
behaviour). The primary working assumption is that behavioural choice can be represented as 
an ordinal outcome (skipping < help-seeking < self-reliance). RQ2 evaluates whether this 
ordinal representation is adequate under proportional odds or whether selected predictors 
require category-specific effects. Trait- and state-level math attitudes will be included as 
predictors. No special actions will be taken by the experimenter for participants showing 
extreme patterns (e.g., skipping all items). Such behaviour is discouraged by two task design 
features: (1) a mandatory 15-second dwell time per item after each choice, and (2) automated 
prompts every 5 consecutive skips or help requests, reminding participants of all the available 
behavioural choices. If the hypothesised ordinal structure proves clearly inadequate, additional 
follow-up analyses may be conducted to explore whether a categorical specification provides a 
more appropriate representation of the data. These analyses are not part of the preregistered 
decision pipeline, are not implemented in the current analysis script, and will be reported 
explicitly as supplementary, non-preregistered analyses.

Sampling 
Data collection procedures 
Participants are recruited by initially contacting the Heads of Public Primary Schools in middle-
income areas of a Western European country. Once the teachers grant permission, parental 
consent is requested. The study involves students in the 4th and 5th grades, aged 9 to 11 years. 
Exclusion criteria include formal diagnoses of intellectual disabilities or neurological/genetic 
conditions. No financial compensation will be provided. The study has received approval from 
the Authors’ University ethics committee for psychological research. Data collection takes 
place during the second half of academic year (2025-2026). The first session (T0) is conducted 
starting in early March 2026, followed by the second session (T1), which will be conducted 
between mid-April and late May 2026.

Sample size

The sample size is partially constrained by available resources. We aim to recruit approximately 
35-40 classes, corresponding to an initial target of about 500 students. We anticipate a missing 
rate of approximately 8% per session, due primarily to school absences (on average, 1-2 out of 
20 children per class on a given testing day). Additionally, we estimate that approximately 15-20 
children per class will meet the inclusion criteria and return the signed parental informed 
consent. Based on these factors, we expect to include 400 eligible participants in the final 
analysis. Recruitment will continue until 400 eligible participants are obtained or all recruited 
classes complete both sessions, whichever occurs first. If resources limit recruitment below 
400, analysis proceeds with the achieved N ≥ 300, where simulations confirm > 90% power 
(ordinal conceptualization) or adequate Monte Carlo-based standard-error precision for the 
primary parameters (categorical conceptualization) at N=300. 
Sample size rationale 
Two simulation-based sample-size analyses were conducted: one for the primary ordinal 
analysis and one for a possible multinomial robustness analysis in case the ordinal 
representation proves inadequate. Full details and the power analysis code are provided in the 
attached files “PowerAnalysisOrdinal.R” (ordinal conceptualization) and 
“FastPowerCategorical.R” (categorical conceptualization). In both cases, the power analyses 
are fully aligned with the planned statistical models outlined in the “Analysis Plan section” and 
are based on data-generating processes consistent with the large simulated dataset attached in 
“Study Design section”. The model run in both power analyses includes all the possible 
predictors and the covariates to be as conservative as possible. The following paragraphs 
provide a concise overview of both power analysis procedures and their results. 1) Power 
analysis for the ordinal conceptualization of the dependent variable (math-related behavioural 
choices) was evaluated using a simulation-based approach with cumulative link mixed models 
(CLMM). The power analysis implemented in “PowerAnalysisOrdinal.R”, was based on a power 
simulation with 5,000 iterations Monte Carlo simulation iterations of the full model, including 
predictors relevant to both RQ1 and RQ2. The simulated logistic linear regression model tested 
the effects of standardized predictors (buoyancy, avoidance, worry, perceived competence) on 
ordered behavioural choices collected at T1 (skipping < help-seeking < self-reliance), while 
controlling for all relevant covariates and random intercept, thus accounting for intersubjective 
variability. Under the alternative hypothesis, based on coefficients typically reported in 
literature, betas were set to -0.25 for worry, -0.30 for avoidance, 0.30 buoyancy, and 0.25 for 
perceived competence, and a subject-level random intercept variance of tau = 1, reflecting 
moderate individual differences between subjects in baseline behavioural choice disposition. 
These values correspond to statistically small effects, consistent with those typically observed 
in psychological research (odds ratios ≈ 0.78 for worry/avoidance, ≈ 1.35 for buoyancy/self-
competence per 1 SD), equivalent to an effect size of Cohen's d ≈ 0.17 using standard logistic 
conversion formulas. The simulation incorporated the planned multilevel structure of the study: 
students aged 9-11 years (target N=400), items nested within students (64 items total, 32 per 
session with T1 as outcome), and realistic trait-state correlations via a multivariate covariance 
matrix. Results for H1 indicated power exceeding 80% for detecting each individual predictor 
effect at alpha = 0.05 (directional, one-sided tests) at N=300. Joint power for detecting all four 
predictors significant simultaneously was approximately 83% without correction, remaining at 
~83% after false discovery rate (FDR) correction across the four predictors (buoyancy, 
avoidance, worry, perceived competence). In order to prove that the simulated process is valid 
and well-calibrated, null hypothesis (H0) simulations have been conducted with all betas = 0, 
confirming Type I error rates near 5%. This result demonstrated no true effects present under 

PAGE 5
the null hypothesis scenario, confirming no inflation of false positives. 2) Power analysis for the 
categorical conceptualization was conducted using simulation-based methods with multilevel 
multinomial logistic regression (brms). Due to excessive computational demands for the full 
model, a streamlined power analysis was implemented. The power analysis implemented in 
“FastPowerCategorical.R” simulates data across a grid of sample sizes (N=100 to 500 in steps 
of 50), averaging 10 replications per N to compute variance-covariance matrices of key 
posterior parameters for RQ2. The simulated model tested the effects of standardized 
predictors (buoyancy, avoidance, worry, and perceived competence) on categorical behavioural 
choices at T1 (skip, help-seeking, self-reliance), while controlling for prior math ability at T0, 
math anxiety, self-competence, gender, and random intercepts. Under the alternative 
hypothesis (H1), logit coefficients for key contrasts were set to reflect plausible shifts in choice 
probabilities For skip vs. (help-seeking/self-reliance): buoyancy β= -0.30, avoidance β= +0.30, 
worry β= +0.25, perceived competence β= -0.25. For help-seeking vs. self-reliance: smaller 
effects in all the considered predictors (e.g., buoyancy β= -0.15). These yield odds ratios which 
will range ≈ 0.74-1.35 per 1 SD across primary contrasts, equivalent to an effect size of Cohen's 
d ≈ 0.17 using standard logistic conversion formulas. As before, the simulation incorporated the 
planned multilevel structure of the study, with items nested within participants (32 items per 
participant at session 2 as outcome, across approximately 400 participants aged 9-11 years), 
and realistic trait-state correlations via a multivariate covariance matrix. Power analysis 
prioritized the four primary contrasts central to HP2 (effects of buoyancy, avoidance, worry, and 
perceived competence on self-reliance vs. skipping behaviour). Secondary help-seeking 
parameters were monitored for completeness, focusing especially on one additional parameter 
(buoyancy effect on help-seeking). To determine a plausible sample size, we first identified 
target standard errors for the primary effects after Bonferroni correction across five predictors, 
corresponding to 80% power. Across a simulation grid (N=100-500), posterior SEs for the four 
primary parameters fell below target thresholds (ranging from 0.035-0.079) by N=300: effects of 
buoyancy (0.033), avoidance (0.016), perceived competence (0.032), and worry (0.067). The 
secondary parameter (buoyancy effect on help-seeking) remained slightly elevated (0.068) 
compared to its stringent target (0.035) due to its smaller effect simulated (β=0.15) and the 
conservative Bonferroni correction across five predictors, which is known to reduce power for 
smaller effects. In this context, MCMC is used to obtain stable coefficient and standard-error 
estimates for complex multinomial mixed models. The resulting quantities are used for 
approximate frequentist evaluation of precision and test performance, not for Bayesian 
posterior inference. Further details on these assumptions and the complete R code for the 
power analysis can be found in the attached files “PowerAnalysisOrdinal.R” and 
“FastPowerCategorical.R”.

Measured variables 
The experiment includes three main sets of measured variables: math performance, math 
behavioural choices, trait- and state-level math attitudes. Math Performance: is assessed in 
both sessions (T0 and T1) using two parallel digital algebraic tasks each consisting of 32 
different problems (e.g., 34 - ? = 21). A pool of 100 arithmetic problems has been generated 
using a custom R script, subject to several constraints (e.g., operands ranging from 12 to 99, 
exclusion of multiples of 10, and balanced positioning of the unknown term across items). The 
generated pool has been subsequently reviewed and fine-adjusted. For each participant, 32 
problems per session are randomly sampled from this pool. To prevent repetitions, the sampling 
algorithm excludes problems already presented in T0 when drawing items for T1. For each item, 
students have a time limit of 25 seconds, determined through a prior pilot test, to solve the 
problems and enter the correct answer using the keyboard. 1 point will be assigned for correct 
and timely answers, while 0 points for incorrect, late or skipped responses. Math behavioural 
choices: will be assessed only in the second session (T1). For each item, participants will select 
one of three task-related options: (1) not solving the problem, indicating skipping behaviour; (2) 
requesting a hint, that provides a brief problem-solving strategy (e.g., for “? – 34 = 16”, the hint 
would be “Try using the strategy: 34 + 16 = ?”), indicating help-seeking behaviour; or (3) solving 
the problem independently, reflecting self-reliance behaviour. For each item, the selected 
behavioural choice yields one categorical choice variable with three levels (skip, help or self), 
yielding an objective measure of students’ observable behavioural choices adapted from the 
paradigm proposed by Wang et al. (2025). Trait measures will be assessed using the following 
self-report questionnaires: - Math anxiety: will be measured using the Abbreviated Mathematics 
Anxiety Scale for Children (AMAS, Caviola et al., 2017). The 9-item measure assesses 
participants’ anxiety levels towards mathematics in different scenarios on a 5-point Likert scale, 
with higher scores indicating greater math anxiety. - Academic Buoyancy: will be measured 
using the Academic Buoyancy Scale (Martin & Marsh, 2008). The 4-item questionnaire 
measures students’ ability to cope with everyday academic challenges using a 7-point Likert 
scale, with higher scores indicating greater academic buoyancy. - Math avoidance: will be 
assessed using an adapted version of the Math Avoidance Scale (Conlon et al., 2022). The 
questionnaire measure participants’ avoidant thoughts or behaviours in 10 math-related 
situations on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater math avoidance. - Math 
Self-competence: will be assessed using an adapted version of Harter's questionnaire (2012), 
measuring scholastic self-competence in the mathematical domain. The final 10-item version 
measure uses a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater math self-
competence. An additional variable is also collected at T0, namely trait test anxiety; however, 
this is not included or analysed in the present study and will be used for other purposes in a 
different study. State-level math attitudes will be assessed using the following self-report 
questionnaires administered in close temporal proximity to math performance at T0. - Worry: 
will be assessed using three adapted items from Mammarella et al. (2023). Items measure 
participants’ worry levels during the math task on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores 
indicating greater worry. - Perceived Competence: will be assessed using three adapted items 
from Mammarella et al. (2023). Items measure participants’ perceived competence during the 
math task on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence.

Analysis Plan 
Statistical models 
This section provides a brief overview of the data analysis steps and modelling approach. The 
full analysis pipeline, including data preparation, model specifications, and hypothesis tests, is 
implemented in the attached R script “PrePlannedAnalysis.R”, which is applied to a simulated 
dataset ("SimulatedDatasetBehaviours.csv"). Statistical modelling and hypothesis testing Prior 
to structural modelling, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) will be conducted to evaluate the 
psychometric adequacy of buoyancy and avoidance as latent constructs. Initial model fit will be 
evaluated against the pre-specified criteria (CFI > .95; RMSEA < .08). If the initial model does not 
meet these criteria, modification indices may be inspected in a limited and theory-constrained 
way. At most two correlated residuals will be added, and only when they involve item pairs with 
clear substantive or wording overlap. Any such re-specification will be documented explicitly 
and treated as a pragmatic measurement adjustment rather than as confirmatory evidence for 
the revised measurement model. Reliability will be evaluated using ordinal alpha and treated as 
a descriptive indicator of measurement adequacy rather than as a strict pass/fail criterion. 
Values clearly below conventional standards will be discussed as a limitation and may motivate 
sensitivity analyses excluding the corresponding construct. Additionally, prior to including both 
constructs in the structural model, multicollinearity between buoyancy and avoidance will be 
assessed; if the inter-construct correlation exceeds |r| > .70, the two predictors will not be 
entered simultaneously in the same primary model. In that case, separate sensitivity models 
will be estimated for each construct, and the main confirmatory inference will be restricted to 
predictors that can be included without problematic redundancy. If one or both constructs fail 
the pre-specified fit criteria, the corresponding variable(s) will be excluded from all structural 
models, and this exclusion will be transparently reported. In this case, the primary analysis will 
proceed with the retained predictors only. RQ1. The model-building strategy then proceeds in 
three sequential steps. First, within the ordinal family, the predictor structure will be established 
through nested cumulative logit mixed-effects models estimated in brms. The baseline model 
(M0) includes only state-level predictors (worry and perceived competence), the covariates 
(prior math ability at T0 and gender), and a random intercept for participant. This constitutes the 
full mediation model. M1 extends M0 by adding buoyancy and avoidance as direct predictors of 
choice, conditional on satisfactory psychometric screening and acceptable multicollinearity. 
M2 extends M1 by adding direct paths from math anxiety and self-competence to choice, 
thereby testing partial against full mediation. M2 will be estimated only if M1 is preferred over 
M0. RQ2. Starting from the Stage-1 selected ordinal model, category-specific extensions will be 
evaluated by relaxing the proportional-odds constraint for selected predictors and comparing 
each extension against its predecessor via LOO. In the current analysis script, this step is 
implemented as a worked example and template for the same comparison logic. Each 
extension will be compared against its predecessor via LOO, and a category-specific 

PAGE 8
parameterisation will be retained only if the more complex model is clearly preferred. The model 
retained at the end of the preregistered selection pipeline will be used for the inferential tests, 
and all inferential results will be reported as conditional on that selected model. For the 
selected best-fitting model, directional inferences will be conducted for each retained primary 
predictor. For predictors satisfying proportional odds, a single coefficient will be interpreted. For 
predictors with category-specific effects, coefficients will be interpreted separately. Because 
the analysis combines preregistered model selection with MCMC-based estimation, all 
inferential results will be interpreted within a pragmatic frequentist framework and treated as 
conditional on the selected model. MCMC is used here as a computational device to 
approximate parameter estimates and standard errors in models for which simpler estimation 
routines are not readily available, not as a basis for Bayesian inference. Multiplicity adjustment 
will be applied only across the retained primary directional effects using the FDR procedure. 
Secondary and exploratory parameters will be reported descriptively and clearly distinguished 
from the preregistered primary effects.

Transformations 
Data will be imported and pre-processed to include trials from T1, which constitute the 
outcome of interest. Measures of math performance and math attitudes assessed both at T0, 
including both trait- and state-level variables, as well as additional trait-level measures 
collected at Session 2 (T1), will be merged with the behavioural data and included as predictors 
or covariates. For each item at T1, a single outcome variable (choice) will be derived, indicating 
the behavioural option selected by the student (skipping, help-seeking, self-reliance). All 
continuous predictors will be standardized (z-scores).  
Inference criteria 
To address both RQs, the model with higher ELPD under Pareto-smoothed LOO-CV will be 
selected as best-fitting (see "PrePlannedAnalysis.R"). Specifically, the differential ELPD must 
exceed twice its standard error, consistent with conventional LOO-CV thresholds (Vehtari et al., 
2017). This applies to all pairwise model comparisons in the two-stage model-building 
procedure: M0 vs. M1, M1 vs. M2 (Stage 1), and each successive extension vs. its predecessor 
(Stage 2). At each step, if the more complex model is not clearly preferred, the simpler model is 
retained, and the pipeline does not proceed to the next extension. To address RQ1, effects will 
be evaluated using one-tailed p-values based on MCMC-estimated coefficients and their 
associated standard errors. For the primary directional hypotheses, p-values < .05 after 
multiplicity correction will be treated as statistically significant. These p-values are interpreted 
as approximate frequentist quantities, conditional on the selected model, and should be read 
with caution given the data-dependent model-selection procedure. Accordingly, the inferential 
claims are modest: statistical significance is used as one decision criterion, not as definitive 
evidence, and residual inflation of false positives cannot be ruled out. Directional expectations 
are specified a priori: positive coefficients expected for academic buoyancy and perceived 
competence (reflecting a higher probability of self-reliant behaviour relative to help-seeking and 
skipping), and negative for avoidance and worry (reflecting a higher probability of skipping 
relative to help-seeking and self-reliant behaviour). If one or both constructs fail the pre-

specified fit criteria, the corresponding variable(s) will be excluded from all structural models, 
and this exclusion will be transparently reported. In this case, the primary analysis will proceed 
with the retained predictors only. For the selected best-fitting model, directional hypothesis 
tests will be conducted for each primary predictor retained in the final model. The number of 
primary comparisons ranges from two to four, depending on the inclusion of buoyancy and 
avoidance (conditional on measurement model fit). Since RQ1 tests, potentially, all four 
predictors simultaneously, p-values for the preregistered primary directional effects retained in 
the final selected model will be adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. We 
note, however, that because predictor inclusion and model structure are themselves data-
dependent, this correction only partially addresses the overall false positive risk and does not 
fully account for the full analytic search process. Indirect effects will be estimated by 
multiplying posterior draws from the relevant trait-to-state model (path a) by posterior draws 
from the corresponding coefficient(s) in the selected behavioural-choice model (path b). These 
indirect effects will be interpreted cautiously as conditional statistical decompositions rather 
than as strong causal mediation effects, given the partially time-lagged and partially concurrent 
measurement structure of the study. 
Data inclusion and exclusion 
Participants will be excluded from the primary analysis if they are missing the entire T1 
behavioural task, if they lack the T0 math-ability covariate, or if technical problems prevent 
reconstruction of their item-level choices. Participants showing extreme but technically valid 
behavioural patterns (e.g., skipping all items) will not be excluded on that basis alone, because 
such patterns are substantively relevant to the research question. 
Missing data 
We expect a low rate of missing data for the questionnaire responses, as participants are forced 
to answer every item, with the task being administered on a computer. However, math trials in 
session 2 (32 items) may exhibit higher apparent missingness due to the explicit skip option 
alongside help-seeking and self-reliant choices. Skipped trials will not be treated as missing but 
will be retained as distinct behavioural category ("choice”=skip) in the outcome variable, with 
task accuracy coded as 0. 
Other planned analysis 
To inform future research, exploratory analyses may examine whether demographic 
characteristics are associated with the behavioural outcomes of interest or moderate the 
observed effects. These analyses are intended to be descriptive and hypothesis-generating and 
will be clearly distinguished from the preregistered confirmatory analyses.
